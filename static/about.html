From schematic to breadboard, one part at a time.

AMPierre helps new engineers learn the fundamentals of electrical engineering at the point where they most often get stuck: turning a circuit drawn on paper into a circuit wired on a breadboard. Give it a schematic and it shows you where every part goes, which way round each component faces, and every wire you'll need, then walks you through the build one step at a time.

Background

AMPierre started from teaching students in the lab. As a teaching assistant for Electric Circuits, I saw students who could work through a circuit on paper struggle to wire it onto a breadboard. Analysing a circuit and building one are two different skills, and the second is rarely taught head-on: which holes are connected underneath, which way a part has to face, why a wire has to cross the middle of the board.

I built AMPierre to help students across that gap. It is the walkthrough I'd give at the bench, available to anyone, any time they're stuck.

Skills it covers

How a breadboard is wired inside

Every hole belongs to a hidden strip of metal. Five holes in a column form one strip; the channel down the middle splits the board into two halves that are not connected; the rails along the edges run the full length of the board and carry power and ground. Once that picture clicks, layouts start to make sense.

Nets: what "connected" means

A net is every point in a circuit that must be the same electrical node. Hover over or tap any part and AMPierre lights up its net. That's what you'd check with a multimeter, and it's how you catch errors, such as a missing jumper or a short.

Component orientation

Diodes have a cathode band, electrolytic capacitors a stripe, LEDs a flat edge, ICs a notch beside pin 1, and transistors a flat face. Polarised parts are marked right on the board, on the side each lead actually goes.

Reading real parts

Resistors are drawn with their colour bands, ceramic capacitors with their three-digit code, and every chip and transistor comes with a pinout diagram, so you learn to find the right part in the drawer, not only in the drawing.

Building order

Start with the power off. Place the chips and transistors first, then the other components, then the wires, and connect the supply last. Make sure continuity is right before you switch on. The timeline follows that order, one step at a time.

Tips for learning

  1. Build it yourself. Use the timeline under the board to place one part or wire at a time on your own breadboard.
  2. Predict before you look. Before each step, guess which holes the part goes in. Then check.
  3. Check every node. Hover a part to see its net, then confirm it on your board with a multimeter's continuity setting.
  4. Start Spacious, then go Compact. Spacious leaves room to trace every lead by eye; Compact is closer to how a finished board looks.
  5. Trust the datasheet. Pinouts can differ between manufacturers of the same part. AMPierre shows the common one; the datasheet has the final say.

The layout engine

You draw the schematic or paste its SPICE netlist, which lists which components connect to each other. From there, Python algorithms decide where each component goes. No AI is used in the breadboard wiring itself, though the algorithm was developed with the help of AI. It is tuned for clean, easy-to-read layouts, so students can follow the wiring without getting lost.

The parts are laid out to follow the schematic: parts that hang from the supply rail, transistors and chips in the middle, and parts that stand on the ground rail. Every jumper then runs flat along a row or straight down a column, so none of them overlap. The rare jumper that has to go corner to corner is drawn arched, the way a real flexible wire hops over the board. AMPierre also shows where the bench supply and the wave generator clip on, and it checks every connection before showing you anything, so the board you see is electrically the circuit you drew.

AMPierre isn't a simulator. It shows you how to build a circuit, not whether the circuit works. If your circuit doesn't work, the problem may not be the layout.

Featured build: AJ's three-band audio equalizer

One of my own projects, and the biggest circuit that ships with AMPierre. I designed and simulated it in LTspice, then built it on a breadboard. An input amp feeds three filters (bass, mid and treble), each with its own knob. Three buffers keep the filters from loading each other, a summing amplifier mixes the bands back together, and a peak detector and comparator light an LED when the output gets close to clipping.

When I was starting out as an electrical engineering student, I wished I had a tool like this to help with my breadboard builds.
